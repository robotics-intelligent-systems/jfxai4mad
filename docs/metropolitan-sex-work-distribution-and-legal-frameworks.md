## 1. Extended metropolitan distribution

The legacy note compares reported or estimated sex-work populations in selected metropolitan areas and discusses how activity may be distributed between street-based, indoor and digitally mediated settings.

The figures should be treated as **historical source claims**, not as directly comparable measurements. Different sources may count registered workers, estimated workers, women only, all adults, people contacted by services, advertisements or other populations.

## 2. European metropolitan notes

### 2.1 Spain — Madrid, Barcelona and Valencia

The source cites an estimate of approximately **114,500 women** in prostitution-related contexts, with broader estimates ranging from approximately **152,000 to 184,000** when non-advertised or non-digitized activity is considered.

It also reports the following origin shares for records in which origin was identified:

- Colombia: approximately 28%.
- Spain: approximately 13%.
- Brazil: approximately 5%.
- Venezuela: approximately 5%.
- Unknown or undocumented origin: approximately 37%.

The source further claims that a large share of the market has shifted from street-based settings toward apartments, houses and online advertising. Examples mentioned include the Marconi industrial area in Madrid and the Raval district in Barcelona.

### 2.2 Germany — Berlin, Hamburg and Frankfurt

The note distinguishes between formal registration under Germany's prostitution-protection framework and broader unofficial estimates. It cites roughly **25,000–30,000 registered persons**, while also reproducing much larger unofficial estimates.

The source attributes a significant migrant share to the sector, particularly from Eastern Europe and Latin America, and identifies Berlin and Hamburg as important urban concentrations.

### 2.3 France — Paris, Lyon and Marseille

The source cites national estimates of roughly **30,000–40,000 persons**, with a substantial concentration in Île-de-France. It describes a high foreign-born share, mentioning Nigerian, Chinese, South American and Balkan-origin populations.

These percentages require source-by-source verification because population definitions, observation periods and sampling methods can differ substantially.

## 3. Latin American metropolitan notes

### 3.1 Colombia — Bogotá, Medellín, Cali and Cartagena

The legacy source cites a national estimate of approximately **244,000 persons** and identifies Bogotá, Medellín, Cali and Cartagena as important urban concentrations.

For Bogotá, it refers to district-level or council records covering several thousand people in directly observed commercial zones and describes a mixed Colombian and Venezuelan population. It also distinguishes between street-based activity and establishments or daily-rental lodging environments.

### 3.2 Mexico — Mexico City, Tijuana and Guadalajara

The source gives a broad national estimate of approximately **70,000–100,000 women** and states that health brigades in Mexico City serve more than 15,000 sex workers.

It attributes most of the population to Mexican nationals, including internal migration from southern states, with an additional regional migrant component. Locations mentioned include La Merced, Sullivan, Cuauhtémoc and Tijuana's Zona Norte.

### 3.3 Brazil — São Paulo and Rio de Janeiro

The source reproduces a national estimate exceeding **1.4 million persons** and describes a comparatively high local-national share. It also notes the presence of Afro-Brazilian and transgender populations in some urban settings.

These claims require especially careful validation because broad national estimates may not be methodologically comparable with registration, service-contact or city-level data used elsewhere in the document.

## 4. Comparative legal models

The source organizes sex-work policy into four broad ideal types:

| Model | General description in the legacy source | Examples cited |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Regulation / legalization | Legal activity subject to licensing, taxation, registration or designated zones | Germany, Netherlands, Austria |
| Abolitionist / Nordic-model variants | Sellers are not criminalized while buyers and/or intermediaries may face penalties | Sweden, France, Ireland; Spain described as transitional in the legacy note |
| Decriminalization | Consensual adult sex work is removed from criminal-law prohibitions and addressed through ordinary labor/public-law rules | New Zealand |
| Partial prohibition / legal ambiguity | Direct exchange may not itself be criminalized, while public-space, zoning, public-order or establishment rules constrain practice | Various Latin American jurisdictions in the legacy source |

These categories are simplifications. Actual law can differ by municipality, licensing regime, immigration status, third-party involvement, advertising, public-space rules and anti-trafficking law.

## 5. European regulatory approaches

### 5.1 Regulation / labor-rights approach

The legacy source describes Germany, the Netherlands and Austria as examples in which some forms of sex work may operate within formal legal and tax frameworks.

Common elements described include:

- licensing or registration;
- taxation;
- health or social-insurance obligations;
- designated establishments or tolerance zones;
- municipal and land-use controls.

The source also notes a policy concern: registration and documentation requirements may exclude undocumented migrants or push some activity outside formal systems.

### 5.2 Abolitionist or demand-focused approach

The legacy note describes Sweden, France and Ireland as examples of demand-focused models in which purchasing sexual services and/or certain intermediary conduct may be penalized while the person selling sex is not criminalized for the sale itself.

The stated policy objective is to reduce demand and intermediary profit. The source also raises a contested concern that displacement toward private or online settings may make outreach and inspection more difficult.

Because legal structures and enforcement practices differ materially across countries, these characterizations should not be treated as interchangeable.

## 6. Latin American legal and administrative approaches

The source characterizes Colombia, Mexico, Brazil and Peru as jurisdictions where consensual adult exchange may not be directly criminalized in all circumstances, yet public-order, zoning, licensing, policing and municipal rules can strongly affect where and how activity occurs.

### 6.1 Administrative constraints

Examples described include:

- sanctions for public-space conduct;
- public-order rules;
- zoning and land-use restrictions;
- closure of establishments for licensing or planning violations;
- informal or criminal extortion around contested commercial zones.

The source argues that this can create a gap between formal legality and practical vulnerability.

### 6.2 Colombia

The source cites Constitutional Court decisions including **T-629/10** and **T-073/17** as examples of labor-rights recognition in certain contexts involving formal establishments.

These case references should be checked against the official court database and interpreted with qualified legal analysis before being summarized as a general labor regime.

### 6.3 Mexico

The source cites a **2013 judicial decision associated with independent sex workers in Mexico City** and describes recognition under a non-salaried-worker framework.

The exact court, docket, scope, current administrative implementation and interaction with local regulations should be verified before publication.

## 7. Verification requirements

Before using this document as evidence, verify:

1. the year, population definition and methodology behind every demographic estimate;
2. whether a figure refers to registered workers, estimated workers, service contacts, advertisements or another population;
3. current national and municipal law in each named jurisdiction;
4. the exact scope and current status of cited court decisions;
5. current sex-work, anti-trafficking, immigration, labor, zoning and public-order rules;
6. whether nationality or ethnicity figures come from representative samples or limited administrative records;
7. whether sex work and trafficking are being measured separately rather than conflated.

The document should not be used to infer that a person's nationality, ethnicity, gender identity, migration status or socioeconomic position determines involvement in sex work or trafficking.
