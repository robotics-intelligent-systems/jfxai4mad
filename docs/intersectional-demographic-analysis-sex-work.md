# Intersectional Demographic Analysis of Sex Work

**Focus:** age groups, socioeconomic conditions, care responsibilities, migration and ethnicity in selected European and Latin American metropolitan contexts.  
**Editorial status:** translated and refactored from a Spanish plaintext source added after the previous English documentation migration.

> **Verification warning.** Percentages and demographic claims in the legacy source are not independently validated by this migration. They should be treated as hypotheses or historical source claims until checked against current, methodologically transparent studies. Population categories must not be used to stereotype individuals or infer involvement in sex work, trafficking or criminal activity.

## 1. Intersectional framing

The legacy note argues that conditions associated with sex work in European and Latin American metropolitan areas may be shaped by overlapping factors such as age, income, housing insecurity, care responsibilities, migration status, ethnicity and exposure to exploitation.

An intersectional approach is useful only when it avoids deterministic conclusions. These factors can affect vulnerability, access to services and legal protection, but they do not establish why any particular individual enters, remains in or leaves sex work.

## 2. Age-group distribution

### 2.1 Young adults — 18 to 24

The source cites approximate shares of **28–35% in Spain** and **30–40% in selected Latin American cities**.

It associates this group with:

- comparatively high use of digital channels such as subscription platforms, webcam services and online advertising;
- participation in some street-based nighttime markets;
- heightened concern about deceptive recruitment and trafficking.

The source's claim that this is the age group with the highest trafficking risk requires direct evidence and should not be generalized without age-specific victimization data.

### 2.2 Adults — 25 to 36

The legacy source describes this age band as the largest segment, at approximately **32–40%** of the population considered.

It associates the group with:

- indoor establishments;
- clubs;
- shared apartments;
- agencies or intermediary structures;
- family financial responsibilities and remittance obligations in some migrant households.

These associations should be treated as descriptive hypotheses rather than universal patterns.

### 2.3 Adults over 37

The source cites an approximate cumulative share of **15–25%** and describes possible concentration in lower-income or street-based settings for some workers.

It also raises concerns about age-related stigma, limited access to alternative employment and incomplete social-security contributions.

The threshold of 37 should not be interpreted as a standard demographic category; it reflects the organization of the legacy source rather than a generally accepted age classification.

## 3. Socioeconomic stratification and care burdens

### 3.1 Poverty and housing insecurity

The source claims that a large share of street-based sex workers in Latin America come from low-income populations and notes the use of daily-rental lodging in cities such as Bogotá, Lima and Mexico City.

The underlying policy issue is housing precarity: people dependent on daily cash income may have less capacity to refuse unsafe work, leave exploitative settings or absorb income shocks.

### 3.2 Care responsibilities and single-parent households

The legacy note cites estimates suggesting that a high proportion of women in prostitution-related contexts are mothers, single parents or primary household providers.

This should be verified carefully because studies may differ in whether they sample street-based workers, service users, registered workers, trafficking survivors or broader sex-worker populations.

Care burdens can influence labor-market choices, but they should not be described as an automatic cause of sex work.

### 3.3 Socioeconomic and digital segmentation

The source contrasts:

- higher-income or more digitally connected settings, where individuals may have greater control over client screening, scheduling and location; and
- lower-income street or industrial-zone settings, where exposure to violence, extortion or unsafe environments may be greater.

This distinction can be analytically useful, but it should not imply that online or higher-priced sex work is inherently safe or that street-based work is uniformly coerced.

## 4. Migration, nationality and ethnicity

### 4.1 European metropolitan contexts

The legacy source claims that foreign-born women represent a large share of prostitution-related populations in parts of Spain, France and Germany. It highlights migration from:

- Latin America;
- Eastern Europe;
- Sub-Saharan Africa;
- other non-EU regions.

The document identifies irregular immigration status as a potential barrier to reporting abuse, accessing services or leaving exploitative situations.

This is a structural-risk argument rather than an inference about migrants generally.

### 4.2 Latin American metropolitan contexts

The source highlights two migration patterns:

1. **intra-regional migration**, including Venezuelan migration to Colombia, Peru, Chile and Brazil;
2. **internal rural-to-urban migration** within Latin American countries.

It also describes Afro-descendant and Indigenous women as potentially overrepresented in some marginalized urban zones.

Any claim of disproportionate representation must be supported by representative data that distinguish ethnicity, nationality, displacement, poverty and city-level population composition.

### 4.3 Race, ethnicity and discrimination

The useful analytical question is not whether a racial or ethnic identity predicts sex work. It is whether discrimination, displacement, labor exclusion, insecure migration status or unequal access to housing and services can compound vulnerability.

The document should therefore avoid causal or essentialist claims based on race, ethnicity or nationality.

## 5. Analytical cautions

Several source categories can easily be conflated and should remain separate:

| Category | Why it must be separated |
| --- | --- |
| Consensual adult sex work | Not equivalent to trafficking or coercion |
| Trafficking for sexual exploitation | Requires evidence of exploitation, coercion or legally defined trafficking elements |
| Migrant status | Does not imply sex-work involvement or exploitation |
| Street-based work | Does not automatically mean trafficking |
| Online or indoor work | Does not automatically mean safety or autonomy |
| Parenthood or poverty | May affect vulnerability but does not determine occupation |

## 6. Verification requirements

Before external use, verify:

1. the original study, sample and year for each age-group percentage;
2. the methodology behind claims about parenthood, household responsibility and poverty;
3. whether migration and ethnicity figures are city-level, service-based, police-based or nationally representative;
4. whether statistics distinguish consensual sex work from trafficking and sexual exploitation;
5. current evidence on reporting barriers for undocumented migrants;
6. the local meaning and comparability of socioeconomic strata across countries;
7. whether the cited categories include women only or people of all genders.

No individual should be profiled, targeted or assessed for presumed sex-work involvement on the basis of age, income, ethnicity, nationality, migration status, gender identity or family structure.
