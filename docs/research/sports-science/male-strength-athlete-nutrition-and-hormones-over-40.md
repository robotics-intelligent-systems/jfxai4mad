# Nutrition and hormone claims for male strength athletes over 40

**Reviewed:** 14 October 2026  
**Status:** English source review for general education. It is not a supplement regimen, diagnosis, or individualized medical recommendation.

This note processes the supplemental Spanish source `docs/A partir de los 40 años.txt`. The source proposes protein, vitamin/mineral, herbal, hormone, creatine, omega-3 and collagen products for male bodybuilders over 40. It combines plausible nutrition concepts with unsupported mechanisms, fixed dosing claims and medical conclusions that need qualification. Age 40 alone does not establish sarcopenia, micronutrient deficiency, low testosterone or a need for supplements.

## Evidence review

### Protein and amino acids

The source's central point—that training and adequate protein support muscle—is reasonable, but a fixed 30–40 g serving or 3–4 g of leucine per meal is not a universal requirement for everyone over 40. The ISSN position stand gives a total daily range of **1.4–2.0 g protein/kg body weight/day** for most exercising people; individual needs depend on training, energy intake, health and the rest of the diet. Protein powder is a convenient food product, not a distinct “natural” hormone pathway. A complete protein food or blend can supply essential amino acids; isolated BCAAs do not replace complete protein. The cited target should not be applied without clinical advice in kidney disease or other conditions requiring protein limits. ([ISSN position stand on protein and exercise](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5477153/))

### Vitamins and minerals

The source's broad claim that absorption generally declines after 40, and that extra antioxidants are needed to protect joints and blood vessels, is not established. Supplements are most defensible when dietary intake, a diagnosed deficiency, a risk factor or a clinician's assessment supports them.

Vitamin D supports bone and other functions, but supplementation is not an established testosterone “booster” for men without an indicated need. Vitamin K2 has not been shown to direct calcium away from arteries as a proven clinical outcome. The NIH fact sheets describe nutrient roles and safety limits; they do not support the source's combined D3/K2 promise. ([NIH ODS vitamin D](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VITAMIND-HealthProfessional/) and [vitamin K](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminK-HealthProfessional/))

Natural and synthetic labels do not create a general ranking of safety or efficacy. For vitamin E specifically, the natural and synthetic alpha-tocopherol forms differ in activity per milligram, but that does not show that athletes benefit from taking extra vitamin E. High-dose supplements can increase bleeding risk, and a large trial found a prostate-cancer signal with high-dose use. ([NIH ODS vitamin E](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminE-HealthProfessional/)) The source's recommendation to favor methylfolate over folic acid, and its warning that ordinary synthetic vitamins “saturate” pathways, are not substantiated here.

Zinc or magnesium should not be taken to raise testosterone without evidence of deficiency or another clinical indication. The claim that magnesium lowers SHBG and thereby increases usable testosterone is not a basis for a general recommendation. “Chelated” or “organic” describes a product form; it does not prove a clinically meaningful advantage for an individual.

### Herbal products and hormone precursors

The source's dose table for ashwagandha, tongkat ali, boron and DHEA should not be treated as a validated protocol. Preparations and study populations vary, and the cited doses do not establish long-term safety or meaningful bodybuilding outcomes. NCCIH finds only limited evidence that some ashwagandha preparations may affect testosterone over a short period; it also notes possible liver injury, thyroid effects and medication interactions. ([NCCIH ashwagandha safety and evidence](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/ashwagandha))

DHEA is a hormone precursor, not an ordinary micronutrient. Evidence does not justify using it as an age-related bodybuilding supplement; safety, interactions and legal status vary. The source's claims that tongkat ali reliably raises LH or prevents conversion to estrogen, and that boron reliably lowers SHBG, are presented without adequate evidence and are not endorsed here. Products marketed for bodybuilding or hormone enhancement can also have quality and contamination concerns; a “natural” label is not a safety guarantee.

### Testosterone therapy, anabolic steroids and SARMs

Testosterone replacement therapy (TRT) is medical treatment for appropriately evaluated hypogonadism, not an optimization shortcut for normal aging or a bodybuilding cycle. The Endocrine Society recommends diagnosing hypogonadism only when compatible symptoms/signs accompany consistently low testosterone; total testosterone is measured on two separate fasting mornings, with free testosterone used in specified cases. A value around 300 ng/dL is a commonly cited threshold, not a stand-alone diagnosis for every person or laboratory. LH and FSH help investigate the cause. ([Endocrine Society clinical practice guideline](https://doi.org/10.1210/jc.2018-00229))

TRT can suppress sperm production and impair fertility. Starting and monitoring treatment require clinician review of contraindications and individualized monitoring, including hematocrit and prostate-risk assessment where appropriate. It should not be described as restoring “juvenile” function. Anabolic-androgenic steroids used at supraphysiologic doses and non-approved SARMs are distinct from prescribed TRT and carry substantial health risks. FDA warns that bodybuilding products containing SARMs can cause liver injury and may increase heart-attack or stroke risk; these warnings do not quantify a risk multiplier for people over 40. ([FDA bodybuilding-product warning](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/fraudulent-products/certain-bodybuilding-products-put-consumers-risk-heart-attack-stroke-serious-liver-damage-and-more))

### Creatine, omega-3 and collagen

Creatine monohydrate has substantially better evidence for strength-training performance than the source's hormone-enhancement products. The ISSN position stand describes 3–5 g/day as a common maintenance amount after loading or with gradual saturation. This does not establish a need for every athlete or prove a cognitive-health benefit for all users. People with kidney disease or relevant medicines should discuss use with a clinician. ([ISSN position stand on creatine](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5469049/))

Omega-3 is not an “indispensable” protection against joint inflammation or cardiovascular disease for every lifter, and **2–3 g/day** should not be prescribed as a default. Benefit depends on the indication and product's EPA/DHA content. NIH ODS notes that higher supplemental doses may increase bleeding time and that 4 g/day in some long-term trials slightly increased atrial-fibrillation risk in people with cardiovascular disease or elevated risk. ([NIH ODS omega-3 fact sheet](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Omega3FattyAcids-HealthProfessional/))

Collagen peptides are being studied alongside tendon-loading exercise, but the source's fixed 10 g/day and claim that collagen “reinforces” cartilage and tendons should not be read as proven prevention or treatment. Evidence remains outcome- and population-specific; collagen is not a complete substitute for dietary protein. ([Systematic review of collagen and tendon outcomes](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13028264/))

## Practical interpretation

- Build the plan around progressive resistance training, adequate energy, sleep and a varied diet; consider supplements only for a defined purpose.
- Treat fixed supplement doses and hormone “optimization” claims in the source as unverified, not prescriptions.
- Discuss persistent symptoms of testosterone deficiency with a clinician. Testing and diagnosis are symptom- and context-led; the source's blanket pre-supplement panel and routine PSA recommendation are not universal screening instructions.
- Tell clinicians about all supplements and medicines. Product quality, interactions and country-specific regulation matter.
