# Country Workforce Profiles for Women in STEM: Source Hypotheses and Data Plan

English translation and evidence review of `El universo de recursos.txt`. **Status:** exploratory country-comparison draft, not a workforce census. The source offers qualitative profiles for India, Brazil, the Philippines, Colombia, Mexico, Argentina and Türkiye, but gives no citations, reference years, country-level counts, occupation definitions or sampling method. Its rankings, specialty pairings, percentages and age patterns are therefore **unverified hypotheses**, not findings about women in any country.

The draft further claims that India and the Philippines lead in absolute numbers of women programmers and data/AI analysts; that women make up 15–20% of advanced software programmers and AI algorithm developers in Argentina and Mexico; that female enrollment in industrial engineering approaches 30–40% in Mexico and Colombia; and that women engineers in Mexican aerospace clusters remain below 20%. These figures lack a year, source, field definition, denominator and consistent comparison basis. They are not reproduced as facts.

## Specialty and age narratives

The source describes industrial engineering as a common entry point, growth in mechatronics and energy in Mexico and Türkiye, established technical careers in Brazilian and Argentine energy, and low female participation in aerospace/naval fields. It attributes differences to outsourcing, university scale, cultural bias, manufacturing clusters and state-backed programs. Each explanation needs a country-specific study; none is established by the supplied text. “Aerospace,” “naval,” “simulation,” “AI,” “programmer” and “analyst” must be defined using a consistent education or occupation classification before comparison.

The draft's three age groups—20–30, 31–40 and 41+—and claims of a sharp exit near 35 or only 8–14% women in senior technical/leadership roles are likewise unsupported. Age bands alone do not establish a cohort trend or explain career movement. A valid study would distinguish career stage, age, graduation cohort, seniority, occupation changes, career breaks and leadership status.

## Data plan

Use the UNESCO Institute for Statistics (UIS) Data Browser for sex-disaggregated graduates by field and research personnel; supplement with national statistical offices and labor-force surveys for employment by occupation, industry, age and sex. Use ILOSTAT occupation data where its classifications and coverage fit. Record the exact indicator, country, year, classification, numerator and denominator, source revision, and missingness. UIS notes that international field comparisons may be limited by differences in definitions and coverage. [UIS Data Browser](https://databrowser.uis.unesco.org/) · [UIS: graduates by field of education](https://uis.unesco.org/node/3079560) · [ILOSTAT data tools](https://ilostat.ilo.org/data/)

A global baseline can provide context but cannot validate the source's country rankings: UNESCO reports women at about 35% of STEM graduates worldwide, while a different UNESCO summary reports 26% of employees in data and AI. These measures have different denominators. [UNESCO: global STEM and data/AI figures](https://www.unesco.org/en/gender-equality/education/digital-divide)

**Next task: MAD-STEM-002.** Build a seven-country evidence matrix with comparable graduate and employment indicators, separately reporting education, occupation, industry, age and seniority. Preserve “no comparable data” rather than filling gaps with estimates. Only after data and metadata are checked should the project consider country-specific talent-development priorities. This note makes no hiring, sourcing or country-level capability decision.
